Power electronic loads draw non-sinusoidal currents. The resulting harmonic distortion at the point of common coupling must meet IEEE 519-2022 limits — or the facility is non-compliant at the meter.

The Problem

Where harmonics come from

Switching power supplies, UPS rectifiers, and variable-frequency drives all draw current in pulses rather than continuously. That pulsed waveform is a distorted sinusoid — mathematically equivalent to a sum of the fundamental plus higher-frequency components at integer multiples of 60 Hz.

A six-pulse rectifier — the topology found in most UPS systems — draws current in two pulses per half-cycle. By Fourier decomposition, its line current contains a fundamental component plus characteristic harmonics at orders h = 6k ± 1 (k = 1, 2, 3, …): the 5th, 7th, 11th, 13th, 17th, 19th, and so on.

Under the idealised assumption of rectangular current pulses and no commutation overlap (Mohan, Power Electronics), the amplitude of each harmonic is Ih = I1 / h, giving a theoretical total harmonic distortion of approximately 29% relative to the fundamental.

Real installations exceed this when multiple loads operate simultaneously. Harmonic currents from different rectifiers can add coherently depending on phase angle, increasing distortion at the common bus.

Compliance Limit

IEEE 519-2022: the applicable standard

IEEE 519-2022 sets harmonic current limits at the point of common coupling (PCC) — the metering boundary between the utility and the customer. Limits are indexed by the short-circuit ratio ISC / IL, which reflects the relative stiffness of the supply.

The relevant metric is Total Demand Distortion (TDD) — the rms harmonic current (orders 2 through 50) normalised to the maximum demand load current IL at the PCC, expressed as a percentage:

TDD = √(Σ Ih²) / IL × 100 %

For the ERCOT reference design (ISC / IL ≈ 17, tier < 20), the limit is TDD ≤ 5 %. Individual harmonic limits apply on top of this: 4 % for orders below the 11th, 2 % for orders 11–16, and 1.5 % for orders 17–22.

Compliance is assessed over 10-minute demand intervals and applies continuously during normal operation, not only at peak load.

TDD differs from THD. TDD normalises to maximum demand current IL, not to the fundamental I1. At part load, the denominator stays fixed, so TDD can be lower than THD even with identical harmonic content. IEEE 519-2022 uses TDD exclusively.

HIT Response

Active harmonic filtering via the HIT shunt converter

The HIT shunt converter (VSC2) operates as an active harmonic filter connected in parallel at the node between the series HIT transformer and the load. It cancels harmonic currents before they propagate to the PCC.

The load draws distorted current containing fundamental plus harmonic components. The shunt converter measures this current, computes the harmonic content in real time via the OEM firmware running at 20 kHz, and injects an equal and opposite compensating current:

icomp = − iharm

The grid-side current through the HIT series path then contains only the fundamental component. The PCC sees a clean sinusoidal current, and TDD at the meter drops to near zero — bounded in practice by the converter's current rating and control bandwidth.

In the Reference Design June 2026 v1, the shunt current reference is rated to ±1 530 A peak, set by the power electronics dimensioning of the OEM build.
