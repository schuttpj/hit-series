The Challenge

Grid voltage disturbances at large-load interconnections

Large computational facilities draw power at a scale that makes them material participants in grid stability. Their information technology equipment runs on switch-mode power supplies engineered to tight voltage tolerances that assume nominal supply conditions.

Grids do not always maintain nominal voltage. Faults, switching events, and load imbalances produce voltage sags, swells, and momentary interruptions. Uninterruptible power supply systems traditionally absorbed these events passively, with the facility free to disconnect. Transmission system operators no longer accept that behaviour at this scale.

Grid codes worldwide now impose fault ride-through (FRT) and active power recovery (APR) requirements on large loads. The Energy Systems Integration Group (ESIG) Large Loads Task Force (2026) surveyed requirements from ERCOT, SPP, ATC, and AESO in North America and Fingrid, Energinet, TenneT, and RTE in Europe. EirGrid has published its own FRT/APR Study Assessment Guide for transmission-connected demand facilities under Grid Code clause CC.7.4.3.2. The requirement families are converging: voltage and frequency ride-through, multi-disturbance withstand, and rapid post-fault recovery of active power.

ERCOT NOGRR-282 effective April 2026, is used as the demonstration case throughout this document. It designates qualifying computational facilities as Large Computational Loads (LCLs) and is the first fully codified large-load ride-through standard with a defined compliance test profile, the Model Quality Test (MQT). It is not the strictest case: Fingrid and EirGrid both require 90% of pre-disturbance power restored within 0.5 seconds; ERCOT allows 2 seconds.

How we got here
1
Data centers were designed for internal reliability only

Facility protection and controls were free to transfer to backup generation or uninterruptible power supply whenever grid conditions looked unacceptable. The grid-side consequence of that transfer was nobody's design requirement.

2
At today's scale, that behaviour became a grid event

On July 10, 2024, a 230 kV fault sequence in the Eastern Interconnection triggered the near-simultaneous loss of approximately 1,500 MW of voltage-sensitive load, primarily data centres transferring to backup through their own protection and controls. No utility breaker opened. The load loss drove a frequency overshoot, and the North American Electric Reliability Corporation (NERC) notes a similar event in a smaller interconnection would cause a far larger deviation. ERCOT has recorded comparable events.

3
Grid codes responded with ride-through obligations

ERCOT NOGRR-282, the NERC Level 3 Alert, EirGrid's frequency ride-through and active power recovery assessment guide, and the Fingrid and Energinet requirements all follow from this. Facilities must now prove, by simulation study against the operator's test profile, that they stay connected and recover active power on the operator's terms, not their own.

Assessed at design stage

EirGrid requires the FRT/APR study before connection, based on site-specific controller settings, with re-assessment when settings change. ERCOT requires the Model Quality Test at the point of interconnection. The study examines the facility as designed.

New scope in the electrical design

Ride-through and recovery require equipment, control methodologies, protection algorithms, and protection settings that legacy data center designs never carried. Each carries capex, opex, and reliability consequences.

Gates the project

The FRT/APR study is a condition of connection. A facility that cannot demonstrate compliance does not energise, whatever its internal reliability tier.

Ride-through compliance is not a permitting formality to be resolved after the design is frozen. It shapes the single-line diagram, the equipment list, and the protection philosophy. Projects that treat it as an afterthought discover the gap at the interconnection study, when redesign is most expensive.

The Standard

What information technology equipment actually tolerates: the ITIC curve

The ITIC curve (IEEE Std 1159-2019, formerly CBEMA) defines the voltage tolerance envelope for information technology equipment. It specifies the combinations of voltage magnitude and event duration that equipment must withstand without damage or loss of function.

The curve has three zones. The safe zone is not a single voltage threshold: it is duration-dependent. Equipment rated to ITIC tolerates a complete voltage interruption for up to 20 ms, a sag to 70% for up to 500 ms, and a sag to 80% for up to 10 seconds. Outside those boundaries, equipment protection may activate.

The ITIC envelope is a property of the equipment, not of any grid code. It is the same in Texas, Finland, and Denmark, and it is the fixed reference against which each system operator's ride-through requirement can be plotted. Any FRT profile that extends below the ITIC lower boundary produces the same class of compliance gap, whichever jurisdiction publishes it.

ERCOT NOGRR-282 requires ride-through at conditions that fall below the ITIC lower boundary. This is the core compliance gap: the grid code demands more than the equipment was designed to survive.

The gap is not theoretical. It appears precisely in the ERCOT MQT (Model Quality Test), the 25-second voltage ride-through test profile that every LCL must complete at its point of interconnection.

The Technology

Active series compensation: the HIT between grid and load

The HIT (Hybrid Intelligent Transformer) sits in series between the grid supply and the data center load. Rather than passively passing grid voltage through to the load, it actively injects a correction voltage in series, reducing load-side voltage deviation within the limits of its ±20% duty-cycle range. Where source voltage is sufficient, this may bring load voltage toward the ITIC tolerance envelope. Where the source voltage is zero or the sag exceeds the compensation range, stored energy is required.

This episode demonstrates load voltage regulation, one function of the HIT Reference Design controller firmware. The same platform also provides reactive power and phase balancing. Facility-level battery energy storage system (BESS) dispatch, grid fault ride-through, and synthetic inertia are separate architecture decisions, not HIT controller functions, and are covered in other studies.
